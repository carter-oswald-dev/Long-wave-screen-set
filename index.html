Longwave

Flashes your screen with the same on/off timing that DCF77, MSF, WWVB, JJY, and BPC broadcast over radio, so a nearby radio-controlled clock can read it as if the transmitter were on. Useful if the station you rely on is down, out of range, or if your clock only ever syncs itself and has no manual time-set button.

Placement

  • Set your clock to whichever mode makes it actively listen for a signal (usually a "search" or long-press-set button — check its manual).
  • Turn your screen brightness all the way up and dim the room if you can.
  • Hold the clock's antenna side flat against the screen, roughly centered. Most clocks sense along one edge or the back — try a couple of positions if the first doesn't take.
  • Reception depends heavily on the specific screen and clock. LCD/LED panels vary a lot; there's no guarantee for any given pair.
  • A full sync usually needs at least one full minute (BPC repeats every 20 seconds). Leave it running for a few minutes.

What's actually being sent

  • DCF77 (Germany, 77.5 kHz) — local Berlin time, automatically in CET or CEST.
  • MSF (UK, 60 kHz) — local London time, automatically in GMT or BST.
  • WWVB (USA, 60 kHz) — UTC, as the station itself transmits (most WWVB clocks apply their own configured timezone/DST offset after receiving this).
  • JJY (Japan, 40/60 kHz) — Japan Standard Time (no daylight saving).
  • BPC (China, 68.5 kHz) — China Standard Time (no daylight saving). BPC has no official public specification, but this encoding is now built against a real hardware project's Verilog signal generator and its independent PIC firmware decoder, cross-simulated together and checked bit-for-bit across a full year of dates with zero mismatches — the strongest verification of any of the five formats here.

Why this can work at all

These clocks don't receive real 77.5 kHz–162 kHz radio through your screen — screens can't transmit at those frequencies on purpose. What actually reaches the clock is unintentional electromagnetic and optical leakage from the display switching on and off at the right moments, close enough for the clock's receiver to mistake it for its usual signal. It's the same principle an old 2003 tool used for DCF77 on CRT monitors; this app applies it more broadly and adds the timezone/DST handling needed to get the right local time onto each format automatically, based on your computer's clock.
